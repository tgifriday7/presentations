The Leader the Work Requires

What the series didn't reach.

Brandon Freitag  ·  workthatholds.com  ·  June 2026

The opening case

Two leaders. The same
levers. Different cultures
produced. Precisely.

Culture follows what the leader believes.

One CEO inherited a culture built on trust: people surfaced problems early, said what they believed, understood what the organization stood for. The next used the same levers: promotions, funding decisions, how failure was treated, what was said when the truth was uncomfortable. Different beliefs underneath. Different culture produced.

Not by accident. By design. The lever is always the same. The belief operating it is what differs.

The gap in the series

Five papers described
the structural conditions.
None of it is sufficient.

Everything this series describes depends on a set of beliefs the leader holds.

Incentive alignment, delivery redesign, stewardship, readiness, the disciplines that prevent breakpoints: each of these is available only to a leader whose operating system supports them. When that foundation is present, the work holds. When it is absent, the structures get redesigned to serve whatever is really there.

This paper is about the operating system. Not what organizations need to do. What leaders need to be.

The prior question

Do you believe people
fundamentally want
to contribute?

Douglas McGregor named this in 1960. The labels became shorthand. The distinction did not become less important.

Theory X
People are fundamentally resistant to work. They will do less than required unless compelled. Control is not a failure of leadership but its instrument.
Theory Y
People want to do good work. They underperform primarily when the system fails them. The leader's job is to build conditions for contribution, then get out of the way.
Theory X in practice

You cannot apply your way
out of a structural problem.

The CRO watching 50% of the sales org miss quota responds with more accountability pressure. More plans. More measurement. More reporting.

He does not believe the problem is structural. He believes the problem is insufficient pressure. The lever is fear, applied more precisely.

The structural explanation says something different: 50% quota attainment is not an accountability failure but a signal that the quota, the territory, the product-market fit, or the support architecture is broken. More activity, more fear, eventually more turnover.

50%
Quota miss is a structural signal, not an accountability failure
What leaders have to do

Beliefs are necessary.
They are not sufficient.

The test is what happens when honoring those beliefs is expensive.

01
Absorb the cost of the values under pressure
Culture is built in the moments when a value is tested by something real.
02
Say the uncomfortable thing first
The signal runs from the top down. Candor is not a trait you reward but one you model.
03
Promote the people who tell you what you need to hear
Every promotion tells the organization exactly what kind of behavior leads forward.
Behavior 01

Culture is built in the
moments when a value
is tested by something real.

The leader who finds a reasonable exception does not build culture. They reveal the actual terms.

The organization was watching. They now know what the value means: it applies when it is convenient. Every decision is a data point. The accumulation of those data points over time is the actual culture, regardless of what the values document says.

The leader who absorbs the cost publicly, without editorializing, demonstrates that the value is not contingent.

Behavior 02

The signal runs
from the top down.

The leader who names the problem before anyone else does gives explicit permission for the people below them to do the same.

Leaders who manage perception upward while expecting transparency downward do not get transparency. They get very polished reporting.

The actual problems travel sideways, or do not travel at all.

Behavior 03

Every promotion tells
the organization what
behavior leads forward.

More powerfully than any stated value or leadership principle, because it is the decision that determines people's futures.

Leaders who promote the political operator teach the organization to produce political operators. Leaders who promote the person who surfaced the problem no one wanted to name teach the organization to surface problems.

Doing this requires promoting someone who made you uncomfortable because they were right, over someone who made you feel confident because they were convenient. Those two people are rarely the same person.

The structural problem

Good leaders still fail
to sustain culture at
a meaningful rate.

Not because their beliefs were wrong. Because the structural environment rewards something else.

Quarterly pressure
The cost of investing in culture is visible immediately. The return is not. Most leaders eventually stop defending it at that cost.
PE muscle memory
Leaders who build careers inside PE-backed companies develop specific habits. The playbook does not always fit the next context.
Fear as a tool
It looks like leadership. It produces activity. It does not produce the conditions where transformation holds.
No counter-model
Leaders who have never seen a different approach from lived experience have no internal reference for what it produces.
How the model spreads

Jack Welch did not simply
happen to one company.
He happened to a generation.

Forced ranking. Relentless performance pressure. Cut the bottom ten percent regardless of absolute performance.

The model spread because it was legible, attributable, and produced results that could be pointed to in a quarter. It was adopted across industries. The consequences came later.

GE's long decline is the deferred bill: financial engineering, leadership attrition, institutional brittleness. The bill arrived quietly, diffusely, decades later. The feedback never reached the source. The lesson the system taught was: this works.

What works instead

Three interventions that
protect culture structurally.

Selection
The reference call that matters asks: tell me about a time this person told a truth that cost them something.
Governance architecture
Does the structure protect the CEO when doing the right thing is expensive? Board composition, ownership structure, and incentive cycles all matter.
The belief audit
Almost never asked before the hire. It reveals itself in the decisions a leader has made over time when belief was tested by cost.
Structural protection in practice

Culture left unprotected
by governance will eventually
be reshaped by whoever holds power.

The companies that sustain culture over time have structural protection for it.

Costco
Capped margins and above-market wages since founding. This is a governance outcome: board composition, ownership structure, and founder-influenced operating philosophy established to make the culture structurally durable.
Patagonia
Transferred to a trust structure in 2022 that made shareholder return pressure structurally impossible. Not a culture initiative. A governance decision that removed the structural force that would eventually have compromised the mission.
What the analysis cannot reach

Five papers named every
structural failure mode.
This is what sits underneath.

Organizations do not have beliefs. Leaders do. And the organization that gets built, the culture that forms, the transformation that holds or collapses: all of it is downstream of what the person with power believes about the people they lead.

What do you believe about the people you lead, and are you willing to act on it when it costs you something?

The
Foundation.

Organizations do not have beliefs. Leaders do.

Every lever available to a leader is a daily answer to that question: who gets promoted, what gets funded, what failure is treated as, what gets said when the truth is uncomfortable. The accumulation of those answers over time is the culture.

What do you believe about the people you lead, and are you willing to act on it when it costs you something?
